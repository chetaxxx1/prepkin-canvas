## fin-4 Credit scores, plainly (11 cards)
1 painting p01-keys: landlord slides keys across a desk after checking a laptop · "A credit score is one number that guesses how likely you are to pay back what you borrow. Landlords, phone companies and banks look at it before they say yes to you."
2 fig-02-gauge · "It runs from 300 to 850. Under 580 counts as poor, 670 to 739 is good, 800 and up is exceptional. The average American sits at about 715."
3 fig-03-inputs · "Five things feed it, and two do most of the work. Paying on time is 35% of the score. How much of your limit you use is another 30%. The other three are small."
4 painting p04-on-time: hands paying a bill on a phone, calendar day circled · "Paying on time is the heaviest input by a distance. One payment 30 days late can knock up to about 100 points off a good score, and the mark stays on your report for seven years."
5 fig-05-utilization · "Second is how much of your limit you use. With a $1,000 limit, a $300 balance is 30%. Under 30% is the usual advice. Under 10% is better, and it counts even if you pay in full, because the statement balance is what gets reported."
6 painting p06-old-card: old plain card in a desk drawer · "Length of history is 15%. That is why closing your oldest card can hurt: it shortens your record and shrinks your total limit. Keep the old card and use it once in a while."
7 fig-07-late-vs-balance · "Here is the surprise. Paying the whole balance 30 days late hurts more than paying the minimum on time. The late mark lasts seven years. A balance is gone the month you clear it."
8 painting p08-report: person calmly reading a printed report · "Three companies keep the records: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. Your bank reports to them every month. You can read your own report for free at annualcreditreport.com, and checking it never hurts your score."
9 key · "Paying on time is worth more than paying it all off. One late payment costs more than a big balance."
10 painting p10-first-card: 18-year-old buying one coffee with a card · "Starting at 18 with one card, one small monthly charge, and autopay for the full balance is the whole game. By the time you need a car loan or an apartment, you will have years of on-time history."
11 check "You can only do one this month. Which helps your score more?" ["Pay the whole balance, 30 days late.", "Pay just the minimum, but on time.", "Close your oldest card to simplify."] answer 1 · why "Payment history is the heaviest single input. A late mark sits on your report for years; a carried balance does not."

## phil-1 Stoicism: control what you can (11 cards)
1 painting p01-stoa: Epictetus teaching three listeners under a stoa (ref refs/epictetus.jpg) · "The Stoics were Greek and Roman thinkers two thousand years ago. Their first move was to split the world in two: the things you control, and the things you do not."
2 fig-02-two-groups · "Your effort and your reaction are in the first group. Those are always yours. A posted grade, a curve, what somebody else scored: all second group."
3 painting p03-epictetus: seated portrait with crutch · "Epictetus was born a slave and walked with a limp all his life. He had less control over his own life than almost anyone. He said suffering comes from trying to control the second group."
4 painting p04-refresh: student refreshing a grades page at night · "A grade is already up. Refreshing the page is second group. Emailing to ask what you missed is first group. Same evening, two very different uses of it."
5 fig-05-energy · "Worry is energy spent on the second group. It feels like doing something and changes nothing on the page. The Stoics were not calm because nothing bothered them. They were calm because they moved the energy."
6 painting p06-archer: archer at full draw (ref refs/archery2.jpg, pose only, different person) · "The Stoics used an archer. The archer controls the aim, the breathing, the release. Once the arrow is gone, the wind owns it. Do your part well, then let it fly."
7 painting p07-traffic: person calm in stopped traffic, listening to something · "You do not control the traffic. You control whether you spend the twenty minutes furious or listening to something. The event is second group. Your reaction is first."
8 fig-08-question · "The whole practice fits in one question. Before you stress, ask: is this in my control? If no, let it go. If yes, do the next step today."
9 painting p09-list: student crossing lines off a worry list at night · "Tonight, list what is worrying you. Cross out everything in the second group. What is left is your to-do list. It is usually shorter than the worry was."
10 key · "Before you stress, ask: is this in my control? If no, let it go. If yes, act on it today."
11 check "Which of these is in the first group, the part you control?" ["The curve the professor puts on the test.", "How many hours you put in before the test.", "What your friend ended up scoring on it."] answer 1 · why "Your effort is yours. The curve and other people's scores are outcomes you can influence at best, never control."

## study-3 Active recall in ten minutes (11 cards)
1 painting p01-blank: student, blank page, pen, notes closed · "Active recall means pulling something out of your head, not putting it back in. The blank page does the pulling. It is the highest-value ten minutes in studying."
2 fig-02-directions · "There is a difference between knowing something and recognising it. You recognise a face you cannot name. Rereading trains recognising. Exams ask you to name."
3 fig-03-method · "The whole method is three steps. Close everything. Write what you remember on a blank page. Open the notes and mark what was not there."
4 painting p04-writing: student writing fast on a blank page, laptop closed · "Ten minutes, for real. Two minutes shutting the laptop and the notes. Five minutes writing everything you can remember. Three minutes opening the notes and marking what you missed."
5 fig-05-study-list · "The part you missed is your entire study list. Everything else is already learned. Most people study the whole chapter again. You only need the gaps."
6 painting p06-rereading: student rereading highlighted notes, comfortable, smiling · "Rereading fools people. It feels smooth and quick, and that smoothness gets mistaken for knowing. A blank page feels awful because it is actually measuring you."
7 painting p07-walking: student walking to class, thinking · "You do not need anything for it. Walking to class, try to list the four things from the last lecture. Whatever you cannot get is what you open the notes for later."
8 fig-08-research · "In a famous 2006 study, students who reread a passage four times remembered 40% of it a week later. Students who read it once and then tested themselves three times remembered 61%."
9 painting p09-struggle: student sitting with the blank page, pencil hovering, thinking hard · "The mistake is checking too early. If you peek the second it gets hard, you have turned it back into reading. Sit in the blank for a bit. That struggle is the part that works."
10 key · "Blank page first, notes second. Always in that order."
11 check "Rereading your notes feels much easier than a blank page. Why is that a warning sign?" ["Easy means it has already gone into long-term memory.", "Recognising is easier than recalling, and tests need recall.", "Notes are shorter than the textbook, so they read faster."] answer 1 · why "Familiarity feels like knowledge. A blank page is uncomfortable precisely because it measures the thing a test measures."

## psy-1 Your brain holds about four things (11 cards)
1 painting p01-scratchpad: a tiny notepad on a big desk, a person thinking · "Working memory is the scratchpad you think with. It holds about four things at once, and that is it. Every thought you are holding right now is sitting on that pad."
2 fig-02-slots · "Every open loop takes a slot. The song stuck in your head. The text you have not answered. The phone face up on the desk. That is three of four gone before you start."
3 fig-03-fifth · "When a fifth thing arrives, something falls off. It is usually the thing you were trying to learn, because it is the newest and the least practised."
4 painting p04-phone: phone face up glowing beside an open textbook · "A phone face up on the desk keeps a slot busy even when it is silent. Part of you is waiting for it. Face down in a bag is a different object."
5 fig-05-chunking · "The trick is chunking: pack several items into one. 8675309 is seven things. 867-5309 is two. A phone number fits because you learned to chunk it."
6 fig-06-sticky · "Before a problem set, write the three formulas on a sticky note. Now they live on paper, and the slots are free for the actual problem."
7 painting p07-one-tab: clean desk, one window open, phone in bag · "Every open tab is a promise to come back, and each promise takes a slot. Close all but the one you are using. One tab is not a style choice. It is a study tool."
8 fig-08-miller-cowan · "In 1956 George Miller put the number at seven, plus or minus two. Later work by Nelson Cowan settled on about four for new, unchunked items. Seven was the number for things you had already chunked."
9 painting p09-brain-dump: person writing a to-do list before studying, relief · "Before you start, write down everything you are carrying: the text to answer, the errand, the worry. On paper it stops taking a slot. This is why a to-do list feels like relief."
10 key · "Four slots. Everything you carry in your head is sitting in one of them."
11 check "Why does a phone face up on the desk hurt studying?" ["The screen light makes it harder to read the page.", "It keeps a slot busy even when it makes no sound.", "It only hurts if a notification actually buzzes."] answer 1 · why "An unfinished thing stays open in your head. It sits in a slot whether or not the phone makes a sound."
